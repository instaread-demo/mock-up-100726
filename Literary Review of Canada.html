Jackie Robinson’s season in Montreal

David Marks Shribman

Royal Treatment: Jackie Robinson, Montreal, and the Breaking of Baseball’s Color Barrier

Sean J. McLaughlin

University of Nebraska Press

296 pages, hardcover and ebook

Before Jackie Robinson was the principal figure in a great American story, he was the central character in a great Canadian story, rooted in a second-floor flat reached by a steel stairway at 8232 Avenue de Gaspé in Montreal and on the grassy expanses of the old Delorimier Stadium at 2101 Rue Ontario Est. It was at these addresses, sometimes forgotten but vital venues in the Robinson saga, that the fabled shatterer of baseball’s colour line was on deck for the major leagues.

To add to the sports film 42, from 2013, and the countless books about the ascent of Jackie Robinson to diamond fame — a civil rights story as much as a sports tale and, overall, a profile in courage before the phrase became part of the English language — comes a comprehensive look at the season he spent in Montreal. That summer, he went about the city without being taunted, stole bases almost at will, and prepared himself for a leap not only from Class AAA International League play to the National League but also from relative obscurity to living symbol.

In Royal Treatment: Jackie Robinson, Montreal, and the Breaking of Baseball’s Color Barrier, Sean J. McLaughlin, director of special collections and exhibits at Murray State University in Kentucky, paints a portrait of a town (francophone Montreal), a time (postwar exuberance), and a team (the Montreal Royals, the top farm club of the Brooklyn Dodgers) that combined to produce just the right conditions for a dress rehearsal for major-league greatness. “Jackie or Rachel Robinson spent a half century reminding anyone who would listen at every opportunity that they had seen a collective, fundamental show of human decency at Delorimier Stadium and elsewhere in 1946 that still counts for something that matters today,” McLaughlin writes, with reference to the star’s wife. “That, in the end, is why this story is worth telling.”

Robinson thus became part of a Montreal era dominated by a controversial mayor, Camillien Houde (earlier arrested for sedition), and another sports hero, the Canadiens right winger Maurice Richard (lifetime CEO of Habs Nation) — forming an unlikely trio for an unlikely date with destiny. In another book, True: The Four Seasons of Jackie Robinson, from 2022, which readers might select as a companion to McLaughlin’s life-and-times volume, Kostya Kennedy captures the setting:

There was a fresh postwar energy downtown, more stock and variety on the shelves — not just in the department stores but also in the smaller shops. Everything seemed to be springing to life: the bookstores and the bakeries (for the first time in years of sugar rationing, icing was allowed on cakes), the boutiques and the sporting goods shops. At McNiece’s by Central Station, the good golf clubs had come back in, and all kinds of baseball equipment, and newly sewn leather footballs like the ones the pros used in the Big Four. In the shoe stores, salesmen worked the window displays. At the newsstands, the comic books sold out.

Montreal loved Jackie Robinson, and he loved Montreal back, a mutual relationship that, it must be said, would have been inconceivable in an American city at the time. Two years earlier, the Swedish economist Gunnar Myrdal had published An American Dilemma: The Negro Problem and Modern Democracy. Even as a high-profile figure in Dodgers spring training in Florida, Robinson had been shunned, or worse. Just weeks after the Royals star had two singles and a double and scored twice, in a 15‑2 defeat of the Jersey City Giants, four Black people — two married couples — were murdered on a remote back road a bit north, in Georgia.

“Montreal’s embrace of Jackie Robinson, underdog outsider, in 1946 fit a pattern that started when American jazz musicians first came to the city during the Roaring Twenties and fell under an established tradition of opening up to talented athletes and artists who had been at least partially rejected or blocked from fulfilling their dreams back home,” McLaughlin writes in Royal Treatment, among the latest baseball releases by the University of Nebraska Press, the not so little (publishing) house on the prairie with a strong list of books on the sport, including Roger Angell’s classic The Summer Game.

McLaughlin is careful to stipulate that Robinson’s experience in Montreal, and Montreal’s experience with Robinson, did not end racism in the city or beyond but instead offered a playbook on how to battle or at least to contain it in a place where racism and slavery had not been unknown in the past. Even so, Canada — where Black and white ball teams regularly played each other, especially in the Maritimes and in Quebec — had long been a haven and safe harbour for African Americans. For generations, sports had been more open to integration than other areas of the country’s daily life. “This mixing was something that happened naturally and with little opposition,” McLaughlin writes, “as fans collectively decided that they preferred watching better-quality play over upholding a nineteenth-century American segregation policy.” Barnstorming Negro League teams contributed to the interest in and acceptance of Black players, including the future Hall of Fame pitcher Satchel Paige.

It was personal character as much as athletic skills that attracted the Dodgers executive Branch Rickey to Robinson. “Rickey saw his engagement to Rachel Isum and religious affiliation to the Methodist church, to which Rickey was a near fanatical devotee, as two hugely important factors that would help keep him grounded when times inevitably turned rough,” McLaughlin explains. Rickey famously said, “I’m looking for a ballplayer with guts enough not to fight.”

There was, to be sure, much to fight, metaphorically if not physically. Robinson faced racism in team lodging, resentment about his fame from teammates, the challenge of playing a minor-league season longer than any he had experienced before, the cancellation of spring-training exhibition games due to the presence of a Black athlete on the Brooklyn roster, and his own sputtering start on the field. (And the Robinsons had to navigate a foreign city where they didn’t speak the language.) But Rickey felt so good about his protegé’s performance and prospects that he swiftly signed two other Black athletes who would play important roles in individual games and in desegregating the major leagues: Roy Campanella, a catcher who in time would be enshrined in the Hall of Fame, and Don Newcombe, who eventually would win the Cy Young Award and garner most-valuable-player plaudits. Not everyone felt as good about all of this as Rickey, of course. “Plenty of bigots had hoped to see Robinson falter so badly that it would put off talk of integration entirely,” McLaughlin writes, “but adding these two players to the organization amounted to a declaration from the Dodgers that the Great Experiment would no longer hinge entirely on Robinson.”

Robinson benefited from and enjoyed a crossover friendship with Maurice Richard: one a francophone breaker of barriers on the ice, the other an African American smasher of obstacles. What’s much forgotten today is how the fledgling Montreal Alouettes also tried to win Robinson over, making a run at the former UCLA All-American football player. It came to naught, but the attempt at signing him was a formidable one.

As the summer of ’46 wore on, Montreal was gripped by a great fear: that the Dodgers would call Robinson up to the big club before the minor-league season ended, jeopardizing the Royals’ run for the International League championship. This was a primeval terror of Montreal baseball fandom. “I do remember that each time one of our heroes abandoned us for Ebbets Field, it stung us badly,” Mordecai Richler wrote decades later. “We hated Mr. Rickey for his voracious appetite.”

In the end, as always in baseball, the numbers tell the tale. Robinson won the International League batting title with a .349 average, he stole forty bases, and he struck out only twenty-seven times — stats that would be admirable in any level of play today.

◆

McLaughlin deftly portrays both Robinson and Montreal, though some informed readers might regret that he did not examine at greater length the contribution of Wendell Smith, the pioneering writer for the Pittsburgh Courier, then perhaps the leading African American newspaper. “Wendell Smith was the man,” Vernon Jordan, a former executive director of the United Negro College Fund and former president of the National Urban League, once told me. “He was the pioneer guy. And he gave Jackie Robinson the advice he needed.”

For it was Smith who conducted a tireless, decade-long campaign to desegregate major-league baseball. “Smith’s prior ten years of efforts in print and behind the scenes to bring down baseball’s color barrier,” Brian Carroll, an expert in Black baseball, wrote in the journal Black Ball nearly twenty years ago, “have been largely overshadowed in the public’s consciousness by Rickey’s bold stroke and Robinson’s uncommon bravery.” Similarly, in Sam Lacy and Wendell Smith: The Dynamic Duo That Desegregated American Sports, from 2025, Wayne Dawkins pointed out, “It was not apparent at that moment, but Wendell Smith was drafting the blueprint for the 1950s to 1960s civil rights media strategy.”

All that was to come, along with Robinson’s career .313 major-league batting average and his status as the first Black athlete to win a batting title, to capture a Most Valuable Player award, and to be inducted into the Baseball Hall of Fame. Back in 1946, he moved through Montreal with the authority and respect accorded principally to members of the Canadiens. On the field he matched the artistry of Mordecai Richler, Hugh MacLennan, and Leonard Cohen. And it is not too much to say that the future Montreal baseball legends Andre Dawson and Tim Raines walked in his footsteps, all the way to Cooperstown.

David Marks Shribman

David Marks Shribman won a Pulitzer Prize for beat reporting in 1995. His books include 50 Great Moments in Pittsburgh Sports.
