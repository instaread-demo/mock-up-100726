A new debate in Brazil weighs a judge's security versus a journalist's source confidentiality

  • By Leonardo Coelho
  • September 30, 2026
  • English
  • Español
  • Português

Summary

A search-and-seizure operation targeting journalist Luís Pablo over a story about Supreme Court Justice Flávio Dino revealed his source and has sparked a broader debate over the limits of journalistic source confidentiality

Luís Pablo, a journalist known in Brazil’s northeastern state of Maranhão for reporting on the rich and powerful, published an investigation on his website in November 2025. According to Pablo, a former governor who became a Federal Supreme Court justice had improperly used state resources for private travel with his family. 

Shortly after, Pablo became the subject of an investigation himself.

The controversy erupted not just over what Pablo reported, but how he obtained the information. Supreme Court Justice Flávio Dino accused Pablo of publishing sensitive information that put his and his family’s security at risk and came from an anonymous source who had acquired it unlawfully. Since then, what started as an investigation over a government official’s possible misconduct has become a broader debate over source confidentiality — and whether journalists can invoke it under any circumstance.

In December 2025, federal police agents said they suspected Pablo of illegally obtaining information about Dino’s travel and security detail. In March 2026, with authorization from Supreme Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes, they searched Pablo’s home and seized his cellphone and computers.

Then investigators found a name. They found WhatsApp messages on Pablo’s devices that pointed to Raimundo Cutrim, a former Maranhão security secretary who is a political opponent of Dino. Investigators said Cutrim had supplied Pablo with photographs, videos and information about the justice’s vehicle and security arrangements. Pablo later confirmed in interviews that Cutrim was his source.

 The police’s investigation reached Cutrim on Aug.11, 2026, when officers searched his property — and put Brazil’s constitutional protections on source confidentiality under scrutiny. Now, journalists are divided over whether Pablo put Dino’s security at risk, and whether using a journalist’s equipment to identify a source sets a dangerous precedent.

A web of investigations

This is not the first time federal authorities had investigated either Pablo or Cutrim. 

In March 2017, federal police detained Pablo and two other journalists on suspicion that they were members of a criminal organization that blackmailed and extorted politicians and business owners. The three were accused of receiving inside information from a Federal Police agent and using it to demand money. Pablo and his colleagues' arrest was overturned a few days later. A hearing about the case will take place in December 2026, when Pablo will face criminal charges for involvement in a criminal organization, corruption, extortion, and obstruction of justice. His defense denies any wrongdoing and challenges the evidence from the investigation.

Cutrim still faces charges of obstruction of justice in a separate investigation connected to a politically linked killing in 2022.

Investigators are now looking into whether Pablo and Cutrim were engaged in a coordinated effort to damage Dino’s reputation. 

Pablo told LatAm Journalism Review (LJR) that after receiving the information about Dino’s private travel from Cutrim, he sought comment from Dino and the Maranhão Court of Justice. Neither responded, he said.

Pablo said the documents showed that the Supreme Federal Court had requested security support for Dino while he was in Maranhão. The court later requested an armored vehicle whose use was restricted to certain officials that did not include Dino, which Pablo interpreted as confirmation that Dino had been using the vehicle without proper authorization. He also said the use of the vehicle was paid for with a government credit card.

Dino said the use of the vehicle was justified. In a written statement published in March 2026, he said his security team was alerted of an illegal operation monitoring his movements, including the license plates of vehicles he used, the number and names of security personnel, and other details about his protection. 

He said the information was subsequently referred to the Federal Police and Attorney General’s Office, prompting the investigation that ended up investigating Pablo and exposing the journalist’s source.

After Pablo’s source was searched in August, investigators said they found a 100,000 Brazilian Reais ($20,000 USD) transfer from a lawyer to Pablo. The Federal Police said they were investigating whether the payment was connected to posts about Dino in Pablo’s website. Pablo denied the allegation, saying the money was a personal loan from a friend to help him buy rural property.

On Sept 22, Pablo’s attorney asked the Supreme Court to archive the investigation linked to his reports about Dino.

Journalists respond 

The Brazilian press’ response to the Aug. 11 search-and-seizure operation was immediate. The Maranhão Journalists’ Union and the National Federation of Journalists (FENAJ) issued public statements criticizing the operation, deeming it concerning and saying it poses a risk to journalism and freedom of the press. 

The Brazilian Association of Investigative Journalism (Abraji) also publicly criticized the operation, saying that anyone, including public officials, can challenge news content they believe is inaccurate, but seizing news production equipment and exposing a journalist’s source is a threat to journalistic independence.

“If there is a need to investigate a crime, it must be done without violating or weakening a constitutional guarantee for journalists — a guarantee that is also fundamental to the very foundation of democracy,” Abraji said.

The case also drew strong international reaction. Pierre Manigault, president of the Inter American Press Association, said it was “particularly serious that the investigation managed to identify a journalistic source by analyzing devices seized from a journalist.”

Flávia Tavares, chief editor of the news outlet Meio, told LJR that Moraes’ March decision to seize Pablo’s computers and cell phone violated source-confidentiality protections and could have implications for newsrooms of all sizes.

“Journalism in Brazil means both [media conglomerate] Globo and a small-town newsroom where a local ‘blogger’ may be the only mechanism available to hold power accountable and expose wrongdoing,” she said.

Source confidentiality has been protected in Brazil since 1988 under Article 5 of the country’s Constitution. It was meant as a shield for journalists after the end of Brazil’s 21-year dictatorship, which was characterized by heavy media censorship. The text on source confidentiality has remained unchanged since then but it has been a topic of frequent debate in Brazilian courts.

But not all practicing journalists in Brazil have agreed on what the takeaways of Pablo’s case are. 

Reinaldo Azevedo, one of Brazil’s best-known political commentators, said on his Metrópoles program that the central issue was Dino’s security, not source confidentiality. He argued that Pablo’s access to sensitive security protocols suggested Dino’s security had been compromised, and raised doubts about whether the journalist was engaged in journalism at all, comparing him to a stalker.

“Not everyone who does something resembling journalism is actually practicing journalism,” Azevedo said. 

Márcio Chaer, the executive director at Consultor Jurídico Magazine, called Pablo a blackmailer and criticized the mainstream media for giving him the spotlight. 

Other journalists have criticized the Supreme Court’s handling of the case. Writing in Observatório da Imprensa, Cristiano Aguiar Lopes argued the issue goes beyond the usual tension between criminal investigations and press freedom. He said authorities should investigate how restricted information was accessed without using a journalist’s devices to identify a confidential source. He cited access logs, security footage and financial records as alternatives to searching a journalist’s devices.

“The government must present concrete evidence of the specific crime and investigate it without using the journalist’s professional records as a shortcut to identify the source,” Lopes wrote.

Amanda Miranda, a journalist at ICL Notícias, a news branch of progressive think tank Instituto Conhecimento Liberta, has written about the case in her Substack and on X, and has argued it raises questions beyond journalism, including the privacy and security of public officials.

Dino was overseeing investigations into suspected embezzlement, corruption and murder in Maranhão, she noted, making him particularly vulnerable to having his movements exposed. After severe criticism in the media regarding the operation that exposed Pablo’s source, Dino declassified three decisions in which he was involved detailing investigations into corruption scandals in Maranhão. 

Miranda said these investigations made Dino “extremely vulnerable” to the disclosure of his whereabouts and questioned whether Pablo should have published details about them. 

“Journalism cannot be used as a tool for committing unlawful acts, nor as an instrument to threaten and coerce public officials,” she said. “I believe there can be numerous discussions about the importance of off-the-record information, but I cannot apply it to this case and do not consider it representative of the risks faced by journalists in their work and in investigations of public interest.”

Pablo has argued that, no matter the circumstances, his source’s confidentiality should not have been violated.

“When it comes to the source, no matter how improperly they obtained that material, the journalist himself has to protect the confidentiality of the information,” Pablo told LJR. 

Regarding the concerns about his story and Dino’s security, Pablo said the public’s right to know must come first. 

“The journalist's responsibility is to determine whether the material that was provided is in the public interest or not,” he said.
